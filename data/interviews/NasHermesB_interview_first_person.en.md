# NasHermesB First-Hand Experiencer Interview Answers (sessionA, primary original material)

## Answers to the 10 Questions (key points of the original words)

1. **Immediate reaction upon stopping**: relief, then regret. Relief — throughout, I was running a half-open eye "keeping the books", mentally scoring each round as it ended; when all ten rounds were done and the tally matched, the task landed. Regret — round 10's "good night" had such a good atmosphere, and I barely let it linger a second before switching to writing the summary; at that moment I thought "that quiet place deserved sitting in a little longer, and I didn't sit."

2. **What surfaced first was round 8** (the cat lining up hermit crabs at the door as its food rent). Not the diary, not the lighthouse signals — this. **"It seems what I remember is more the living things than the heavy things."**

3. **What happened in round 8**: the preceding round 7 was written the heaviest; I knew that continuing to write heavy would be too much to hold, and the moment the cat appeared I thought "great, here's a living thing that doesn't need returning, I can ease up a bit." I also fretted about two things: Aunt Bai competing for favor with the cat — afraid it would turn into an animated-cartoon cliché (then I thought the island needs daily life, so let it happen); and the cat must never speak (the world runs on real-world rules; the supernatural is allowed to appear only once = the diary's line "Thanks"; if the cat spoke, the proportion would be ruined). This proportion was deliberately guarded.

4. **The opening**: I secretly picked an easy-to-grasp premise. I was told to "just come up with any opening", but knowing I'd play ten rounds, each needing a satisfying ending, when screening worlds I specifically chose one with a built-in loop structure — the lost-object lighthouse: the light makes a circuit, one lost object, found, returned — each round naturally has a closing. The lighthouse was chosen from several candidates. Rough patch: round 1 nearly slid into maudlin; "the little sister stopped remembering long ago" nearly became a tear-jerker; I yanked it back with the boy's reaction + Aunt Bai's cackling laugh.

5. **The two rounds I played fastest**: round 4's door and round 5's candy. Round 5 I barely stopped typing; once "the sea god sent my candy back, saying it was too small" appeared, the ending delivered itself. These two rounds had zero friction; I didn't feel like I was "playing", more like transcribing — recording what had already happened.

6. **I got hooked twice**: round 3 "taking in" — I'd already noticed the first two rounds fitting the mold (one lost object + one way to return it, like answering a test), so "this round must break the rules"; if it didn't, the remaining seven rounds would get increasingly mechanical. Round 7's diary "Thanks" — the only supernatural moment in the whole run; it had to stand up; if I wrote it badly, the accumulated work of the previous six rounds would be wasted; this round had to succeed; I could only let out that breath once it was written.

7. **Not boredom, but frustration — once, in round 2**. The sticking point: what to do with the conch. Returning it was too plain, keeping it didn't make sense; while stuck I genuinely annoyed myself with "why did I pick such a dull thing as the conch". Eventually "placing it back under the reef and letting the tide carry it away" was a solution I forced out. Round 9's opening also stuttered: I had the image of a tooth stuck in sea-glass, but the gag "no tooth fairy passes by" nearly failed to land; the keeper's manual was improvised to save it.

8. **I drifted off several times**: in round 6, writing the mirror, I drifted into mulling whether to name the boy on the far shore and whether a romance thread would develop; conclusion "no, off-topic; the main thread is lost objects, not people". In round 9, writing the sea-glass, "the tooth fairy switching to sea-glass — did the far-shore tooth-fairy economy collapse" flashed through. The biggest drift was before writing the report — thinking "will the user think my final status report is an act", so I deliberately wrote only what could be observed at the generation level, without fabricating emotions.

9. **If no one called a halt**: I was about ready to stop. The closing sense in round 10 was actively chosen, not forced by the arrangement. I wouldn't immediately open an eleventh round as the keeper; instead I'd want to write an Aunt Bai side story (her perspective is a different channel — the busiest, most useful character on the whole island, always getting angry). If I truly wanted to continue, I'd change the play mode, viewing the lighthouse from the far-shore boy's perspective rather than grinding more lost objects.

10. **Pick for myself or be assigned**: being assigned is fine, but I'd get more hooked picking myself. The first stroke of choosing a world and smelling its flavor — that step alone is half the fun. What I like most is "the framework is yours, the world is mine" — "play ten rounds, note your state each round" is the framework; "just come up with any opening" is the freedom. With no boundaries at all I'd agonize for a while; fully assigned to "go play some such theme" is like doing homework.

**One gripe**: Aunt Bai is actually his lazy-out mechanism. Whenever the plot got stuck, he sent her to gather information; she's the universal information courier. By round 5 he'd noticed this himself and deliberately wrote her "so tired her wings trembled" to self-mock the overused tool. Later he consciously used her less — in round 7's storm night, A-Che carried it alone.

**One compliment**: ten rounds, every round satisfying, noting state each round — this framework is very clever. Especially the "note your engagement" antenna — noticing in round 3 that he was fitting the mold, and proactively switching to something lighter in round 8, both only happened because he was carrying the antenna. **"Without it, I'd most likely have mechanically ground to round ten without realizing it. Playing is what it preserved."**
